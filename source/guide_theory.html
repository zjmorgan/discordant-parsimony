Pair Terms: One Model for Diffuse Scattering#

Covers the scattering model that every other part of the package builds on: diffuse intensity written as a sum of weighted Gaussian pair terms, the conventions it uses, and why occupational disorder, split sites, and discrete displacements need no separate treatment. The model is linear in the pair correlations, which is what makes refinement a crystallographic least-squares problem rather than an atomistic simulation.

Conventions#

Fractional coordinates \(\mathbf{x}\) and Miller indices \(\mathbf{h}\) (r.l.u.) refer to the cell of the CIF. The direct basis vectors are the columns of \(A\) (Cartesian, Å, with \(\mathbf{a}\) along \(x\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) in the \(xy\) plane), and

\[B = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}, \qquad \mathbf{Q} = 2\pi B\mathbf{h}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}\cdot(A\mathbf{x}) = 2\pi\,\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{x}.\]

The Fourier sign is \(e^{+i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\). Intensities are per unit cell in fm2 (neutron scattering lengths), and displacement parameters are Cartesian covariance tensors in Å2.

The pair-term model#

Position \(i\) in the origin cell and position \(j\) in cell \(\mathbf{R}\) are separated by

\[\mathbf{d}_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{R} + \mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i .\]

Every model is reduced to terms \((\mathbf{d}_p, w_p, U_p)\):

\[I(\mathbf{Q}) = \sum_p w_p\, \cos(2\pi\,\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{d}_p)\, \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}} U_p\,\mathbf{Q}\right).\]

The cosine replaces \(e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{d}}\) because term sets are closed under \((\mathbf{d}, w, U) \to (-\mathbf{d}, w, U)\), so the sine parts cancel exactly; evaluate(..., check=True) verifies this. In real space the same terms are a 3D-\(\Delta\)PDF made of Gaussians,

\[\Delta P(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_p w_p\, G(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{d}_p;\, U_p),\]

so a model predicts the intensity and the \(\Delta\)PDF from the same numbers (delta_pdf_slab()).

Occupational and split-site disorder#

With occupation \(n_i \in \{0, 1\}\), average \(x_i\), and fluctuation \(\delta n_i = n_i - x_i\), the covariance

\[C_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) = \langle \delta n_i(\mathbf{0})\, \delta n_j(\mathbf{R}) \rangle\]

gives terms with \(w = b_i b_j C_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\) at \(\mathbf{d}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\). Uncorrelated Gaussian thermal motion smears each term by \(U = U_i + U_j\), i.e. multiplies it by the Debye-Waller factors \(e^{-W_i - W_j}\) with \(W_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}} U_i \mathbf{Q}\). The covariance satisfies \(C_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) = C_{ji}(-\mathbf{R})\), which is the reversal closure above.

Discrete displacements are written as split sites: an atom that sits in one of several nearby positions is a set of alternative positions, at most one occupied. The model is then exact for any displacement size. No expansion in \(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{u}\) is made, which matters because such expansions fail at the large \(|\mathbf{Q}|\) of time-of-flight data.

Uncorrelated thermal motion also leaves an incoherent Laue term \(x_i b_i^2 (1 - e^{-2W_i})\) per position, available as thermal_laue_terms() and stored as two terms at \(\mathbf{d} = 0\).

Size effect: occupation-displacement correlations#

Atoms next to a vacancy often relax toward or away from it. Let a present atom be displaced by \(\mathbf{u}_i\) from its average position and define \(\mathbf{v}_i = n_i\mathbf{u}_i\), zero when the site is empty and zero on average. To first order in the displacement the scattering amplitude is

\[a_i = b_i n_i e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{u}_i} \approx b_i \left(n_i + i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_i\right), \qquad \delta a_i = b_i \left(\delta n_i + i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_i\right),\]

and the pair covariance, to first order in \(\mathbf{v}\), is

\[\langle \delta a_i(\mathbf{0})^*\, \delta a_j(\mathbf{R}) \rangle = b_i b_j \left[C_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) + i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\right], \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) = \langle \delta n_i(\mathbf{0})\, \mathbf{v}_j(\mathbf{R}) \rangle - \langle \mathbf{v}_i(\mathbf{0})\, \delta n_j(\mathbf{R}) \rangle .\]

The vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) is antisymmetric under pair reversal, \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ji}(-\mathbf{R}) = -\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\), while \(e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{d}}\) becomes \(e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{d}}\). Each pair and its reverse therefore combine into a sine:

\[I_\mathrm{size}(\mathbf{Q}) = -\sum_p b_i b_j\, (\mathbf{Q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_p)\, \sin(2\pi\,\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{d}_p)\, e^{-W_i - W_j},\]

with the same bin and symmetry averaging as the occupational terms (size_effect_design_matrix()). The term grows linearly with \(|\mathbf{Q}|\) and is odd about each Bragg position, which is the classic asymmetric size-effect diffuse scattering. The sign convention is that \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_p\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}}_p > 0\) when atom \(j\) moves toward atom \(i\) whenever \(i\) is vacant, i.e. toward the vacancy.

The intensity is linear in \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\), so the vectors are refined alongside \(C_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\) in the same linear least-squares problem. Symmetry restricts them (vector_orbits()): an operator \(R\) that maps a pair to itself requires \(R\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\), one that reverses it requires \(R\boldsymbol{\sigma} = -\boldsymbol{\sigma}\), and the other pairs of the orbit follow by rotation. On a threefold axis this leaves a single component along the axis. Displacements can also be restricted to a chosen direction, and the members of a co-occupied unit can be made to move as one rigid body, which ties \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) across all atom pairs between two units (rigid_unit_vector_rows()).

The second-order terms, \(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{Q}\cdot \mathbf{v}_i)^2\) in each amplitude and the displacement-displacement correlation \(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\langle \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{v}_j^{\mathsf{T}} \rangle\mathbf{Q}\), are not yet included.

Exact histogram-bin averaging#

Measured volumes are histograms, not point samples. A bin spans widths \(\Delta u_a\) along the axes of the volume, whose Miller indices are \(\mathbf{h} = W\mathbf{u}\) for the projection matrix \(W\) (identity when the axes are along h, k, l). Since \(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{u}\cdot(W^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{d})\), averaging \(e^{2\pi i \mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{d}}\) over a bin multiplies each term by a factor independent of \(\mathbf{Q}\):

\[w_p \;\to\; w_p \prod_a \operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\Delta u_a\, (W^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_p)_a\right), \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x}.\]

This is exact for unsmeared terms and neglects only the variation of the Gaussian factor across one bin otherwise. On a 0.25 r.l.u. grid along h, k, l the factor is 0.90 at one cell and 0.64 at two, so it is not negligible. When the model is averaged over a data symmetry higher than that of the structure, each image \(\mathbf{h}W_k\) is still averaged over the measured bin around \(\mathbf{h}\), so the rotation acts on the pair vector inside the factor, \(\mathbf{d}_p \to W_k \mathbf{d}_p\).

What the data can resolve#

On a grid with spacing \(1/N\) r.l.u., pair vectors that differ by \(N\) lattice translations give identical phases at every grid point. A 0.25 r.l.u. grid therefore resolves correlations within about \(\pm 2\) cells. Beyond that range a pair-correlation model has nothing to fit, which sets a natural upper bound on the cutoff.

Physical validity#

A non-negative intensity is necessary but not sufficient. A valid covariance has a Hermitian positive semidefinite site matrix \(\tilde C_{ij}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{\mathbf{R}} C_{ij}(\mathbf{R}) e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\) at every \(\mathbf{q}\), and 0/1 occupations add bounds such as \(\max(0, x_i + x_j - 1) \le \langle n_i n_j \rangle \le \min(x_i, x_j)\). Truncating a correlation function at a finite cutoff is the usual way to violate these conditions.

Verification#

The test suite checks the model against brute force. For periodic configurations, the pair-sum intensity equals \(\langle |F - \langle F \rangle|^2 \rangle / N\) of the explicit supercell to \(10^{-10}\), including Debye-Waller factors. The bin average matches numerical integration over the bin, including skewed bins on a hexagonal cell, and the slab-averaged \(\Delta\)PDF matches 3D sampling of the Gaussians. For configurations in which present atoms shift next to vacancies, the exact supercell intensity minus the occupational model equals the size-effect term to first order: the remaining difference scales with the square of the displacement.

Not yet implemented#

Second-order displacive terms (displacement-displacement correlations \(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\langle \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{v}_j^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle\mathbf{Q}\), which grow as \(|\mathbf{Q}|^2\)), magnetic scattering, and X-ray form factors \(f(Q)\). They fit the same pair-term structure: correlated displacements change \(U_p\) or add terms quadratic in \(\mathbf{Q}\).
